Portugal Invented Trans-Atlantic Trade — Then Lost Its Lead

The standard explanation blames bad government. Geography tells a more convincing story.

MSc Economic History — London School of Economics

In the late 15th century, Portuguese sailors opened the first sea route from Europe to Asia. The country was briefly one of the richest on the continent. Then England and the Netherlands caught up, surpassed Portugal — and by 1850 it ranked last among its Atlantic peers in per capita income, a position it still holds today.

This is research I did for my master's thesis at the London School of Economics, where I studied economic history. The conventional explanation for Portugal's decline, advanced by economists Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson, is that its absolutist monarchy stifled private enterprise while England and Holland's more constrained governments let merchants thrive. I found that story hard to square with the historical record: Portuguese merchants regularly pushed back against the crown and won, and the government itself repeatedly tried — and failed — to stimulate private investment. The problem wasn't bad institutions. It was bad geography. Portugal's ports were remote, surrounded by rugged terrain, and far from the wealthiest consumers in Europe. Once England and the Netherlands learned Portugal's routes, there was simply no reason to dock in Lisbon.

Portugal's Paradox

In the early 16th Century, Portuguese innovations in oceanic shipping propelled it to rank as one of the richest countries in Atlantic Europe.1 Despite this advantage, Portugal's economy failed to grow at the same rate as the other four countries that developed sprawling trans-Atlantic trading networks. By 1850, Portugal ranked fifth among the five Atlantic Traders in terms of per capita GDP, and continues to rank last, even to this day.2 This article examines why, in spite of its apparent advantages, Portugal fell behind.

One explanation for Portugal's disappointing economic performance contends that the country had institutions that were relatively more absolutist, which reduced economic growth. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson (2005)—hereafter AJR—argue that in Portugal, as in France and Spain, the absence of checks on monarchical power threatened private property rights and suppressed investment in industry and innovation. In these absolutist countries, the king operated long distance trade himself, captured all of the associated profits, and eliminated the opportunity for economic growth.

I argue that AJR's explanation for Portugal's decline is incomplete and propose a new view that emphasizes geography over institutions. My argument stands on two main sets of findings. First, using textual sources on Portugal's political history, I find that merchants in Portugal constrained the monarchy when it opposed them—just as they did in England, for example—and in several instances the Portuguese monarchy itself sought to encourage not stifle private industry, self-consciously emulating successful peers such as England and the Netherlands. Secondly, I find strong evidence of a pattern between geographic characteristics that hinder economic activity and Portugal's economic performance. In light of these findings, I propose a new view of Portugal's economic history in which geography, not institutions, accounts for the country's relative decline.

Section 2 presents the historical background for my analysis of economic growth in the era of trans-Atlantic trade. Section 3 investigates the role of the monarchy in Portugal's trans-Atlantic commerce, first describing the existing historiographical assessment and then presenting historical examples which challenge that view. Section 4 evaluates the role of geographic factors in the distribution of trans-Atlantic commerce across Europe, first developing a conceptual framework of economic growth, and then presenting data on the important geographical differences across Atlantic Traders. Finally, I discuss the findings and conclusions in Section 5.

The Rise of Atlantic Trade

Before the late 15th Century, Europeans could not travel by boat directly from the Atlantic Ocean to Asia. As a consequence, the exchange of goods from Western Europe to China and India had to traverse West-East across the Mediterranean Sea and the Asian landmass. Consequently, at least in part, the wealthiest countries in Western Europe were those with control over Mediterranean ports.3

However, in the 15th Century, political turmoil disrupted trans-Mediterranean trade routes and increased the cost of transportation for imports from Asia. In 1453 the Ottoman empire conquered Constantinople, and threatened tariffs that increased the costs of transporting spices from Asia through the Mediterranean.4

Because the disruption of channels of trade increased the price of commodities like pepper, it created incentives for countries to circumvent the old Mediterranean throughway. If merchants could reach Asia from the Atlantic, for example, they could trade with India directly. In 1488, the Portuguese monarchy funded the voyage of Bartolomeu Dias, to search for such a route. He successfully rounded Africa's Cape of Good Hope, and reached the Indian Ocean from the Atlantic—a first for a European. Later, in 1499, Portugal's Vasco da Gama improved the innovation by establishing a route that reached all the way to India.

After discovering a new sea route from Europe to Asia, Portugal seized the opportunity to purchase spices directly from India and in the year following da Gama's return, the monarchy financed the first of numerous voyages to India from Lisbon. By 1503, using the trans-Atlantic route, the Portuguese could profitably supply spices to Europe at prices that undercut the old Mediterranean suppliers.5

Portugal's innovation offered an opportunity to compete in the trade of Asian imports and supplant previously dominant suppliers like Venice. Reflecting on the news of the Portuguese discoveries, one unnerved Venetian, Giovanni Matteo Cretico, wrote in a 1501 letter that he feared that Portugal's new routes would devastate his home economy:

[If the news of da Gama's discovery is true] the King of Portugal could call himself the King of Money because all would convene to that country to obtain spices, and the money would accumulate greatly in Portugal with such profit as would follow each year for similar voyages. When this news was truly learned in Venice, the whole city was much stirred by it, ...There is no doubt whatever that the city of Venice came to such reputation and fame as it now enjoys only through the sea, namely by the continual traffic and navigations which it was made by the voyages ... the whole world flocked to Venice with ducats to buy spices and other needs, and also placed their goods there.... Therefore, now that this new voyage of Portugal is found, this King of Portugal will bring all the spices to Lisbon.6

The establishment of new shipping routes to Asia did shift the nexus of commerce to the Atlantic from the Mediterranean. However, Portugal's supremacy in this new Atlantic-oriented world would not last forever. As other countries with Atlantic ports learned to navigate the new routes, they began to trade directly with Asia, and later America, themselves.

England, France, the Netherlands, and Spain entered the trans-Atlantic trade, and, over time, their economies surpassed that of Portugal. These five countries (England, France, the Netherlands, Portugal, and Spain) are identified by AJR as the "Atlantic Traders." All of them developed trade networks that connected markets on various continents, based from their Atlantic ports. Though the innovation of trans-Atlantic routes to Asia fueled growth in all five countries, not all of their economies performed equally well. Between 1400 and 1850, the urbanization rate (a proxy for economic welfare) in England and Holland grew from 8% to 38%, while in France, Portugal, and Spain, urbanization grew from 9% to only 19%, and in Portugal in particular, urbanization grew only from 8% to 16%.7

Despite its role in pioneering the trans-Atlantic routes to Asia that brought wealth to Atlantic Europe, Portugal had the weakest performance with respect to its subsequent economic growth. In the next section, I evaluate the role of the Portuguese monarchy in Portugal's economic performance after 1500 and question whether, as prior studies have suggested, monarchical authority constrained Portugal.

Did Portugal's Government Hold It Back?

The Conventional Wisdom

In this section I discuss the prior literature on Portuguese economic history with which this article engages. I first discuss the New Institutional Economics view, as represented in AJR (2005)'s argument about the role of institutions in the economic history of Atlantic Europe. This AJR narrative focuses on government as an essential cause of variation in economic growth among the Atlantic Traders. I then discuss the research that has occurred since, which recognizes Portugal's laggard economic performance while challenging the centrality of institutions.

For AJR, limits on the exercise of authority by the government play a fundamental role in long-run economic performance. In the case of five countries in Europe (England, France, Holland, Portugal and Spain), they argue that slight variations in the strength of the monarchy at the dawn of Atlantic Trade led to significantly different economic outcomes for the countries. "Where 'initial' political institutions (those established before 1500) placed significant checks on the monarchy," they write, "the growth of Atlantic trade strengthened merchant groups by constraining the power of the monarchy and helped merchants obtain changes in institutions to protect property rights. These changes were central to subsequent economic growth."8 In other words, where monarchs were relatively weak (England and Holland), merchants were free to invest in the economy. In contrast, where monarchs were relatively strong (France, Portugal, and Spain), kings could monopolize the trade for the profit of the crown and exclude private individuals from commerce altogether, though consequently inhibiting the growth of their economy as a whole.

The case of England in particular helps to illustrate AJR's point. For English merchants, the prospect of profit from the trade in goods from Asia motivated investors to finance long-distance ventures to buy goods in regions like Asia for resale at a profit in Europe. However the monarchy sought to direct these profits to its own coffers through taxes. Merchant interests resisted this burden from their government and opposed such taxes in parliament. Tensions surrounding these taxes contributed to the eruption of the English Civil War in 1640, which pitted parliamentarians against royalists. The political attitudes of investors in merchant groups during the Civil War correlated strongly with whether or not they stood to gain from the monarch's policies,9 as did the ownership of stock in overseas trading companies.10 The war led to the King's decapitation in 1649, then a temporary Republican government, and ultimately, after the Glorious Revolution of 1688, a constitutional monarchy with a bill of rights through which parliament held authority over the king. The constitutional constraints on the monarch, and the looming threat of a recent king's execution, meant that investors could trust the monarchy to "credibly commit" to not violate the private property rights of his subjects.11 After 1688, the English enjoyed a democratic government, became the first country to industrialize, and grew to have urbanization rates, and higher per capita GDP than any other country in Europe.

No such government reform occurred in Portugal, according to AJR. There, the government stifled private commerce so much that a rebellious merchant class, such as the one that revolutionized England's politics, could not take hold. AJR's assertion draws on numerous earlier historians of Early Modern Portugal. Cameron (1993) contended "[N]o commerce existed between Portugal and the East except that organized and controlled by the state." Furthermore, Diffie and Winius (1977) labeled Portugal's trading apparatus as "state capitalism" and added that "never before or since has one [European government] become the entrepreneur of an entire imperial undertaking and thrown its whole resources into the creation of profits."12 Similarly, de Oliveira Marques (1972) writes "the essence of Portuguese expansion was a state enterprise ... [T]he feudal structure of the country ... was not essentially shaken [and there is] no doubt that a possible enrichment of Portugal through development of trade and industry was forever jeopardized."13 These historians viewed Portugal's monarchy in the era of Atlantic Trade as a uniquely successful government monopoly that stifled growth in the private sector.

Since the publication of AJR's work in 2005, contributions to Portuguese economic history have refined our understanding of the dynamics at play in the country's economic development. On the one hand, new studies have corroborated the conclusion that economic growth in Portugal lagged behind its peers, while on the other hand they have challenged the role of institutions in that decline.

Several recent studies provide new data to affirm the reality of Portugal's poor economic performance. Costa, Palma, and Reis (2014) showed that wealth collected from Portugal's extractive colonial economies contributed to domestic growth in real wages, but that this growth performed worse than in other Atlantic Trader countries.14 Subsequently Reis (2016) and Palma and Reis (2019) re-calculated economic welfare over time in Portugal by compiling a long-run national income data series.15 This revised series corroborated the view that Atlantic Trade added to Portugal's wealth, but that it did so less than for peer Atlantic Traders.

Other recent work has challenged the model of institutional economics advanced by AJR. Sussman and Yafey (2006), reviewing historic data on interest rates, found that "the notion that financial markets swiftly reward countries for the establishment of investor-friendly institutions is not grounded in historical facts both for the case of Britain after the Glorious Revolution, and for other historical examples."16 Furthermore, in 2013, contributors to Questioning Credible Commitment challenged the view that a simple difference of property right law between Atlantic Traders accounted for the difference in their growth rates. In that collection, Irgoin and Grafe (2013) drew on recent work by Besley and Person (2009) to show that the "extractive" Iberian empires of Spain lacked the same means to collect taxation that "inclusive" empires such as Britain enjoyed, questioning AJR's assertion that "extractive" capability constrained economic performance in Iberia.17 Furthermore, Hoppit (2011) has questioned the premise that British rule of law did indeed provide more secure property rights, writing that in Early Modern Britain "property was often heavily taxed, frequently expropriated and, exceptionally, eradicated [...] [T]hese uncertainties over property rights were sufficient for some to question whether property had a sound theoretical basis at all."18

Amidst this re-appraisal of the role of institutions in early modern economic growth, Henriques and Palma (2019) have produced a quantitative evaluation of the quality of institutions in Portugal. They analyzed several quantitative proxies of institutional quality, including frequency of parliamentary meetings, the interest rates paid on governmental loans, and the tax burden. In these sources they found no clear evidence of an institutional disadvantage in Portugal, writing "[E]xplanations for the little divergence among Atlantic Traders which rely on variation in the quality of 'initial institutions' such as that by Acemoglu et al., cannot be correct."19

The present paper contributes to this revision of the role of institutions in Europe's economic history. It complicates AJR's institutional theory and supports revised interpretations like that of Henriques and Palma (2019) by challenging the central role of institutions. As I show below, Portuguese merchants competed successfully with the Portuguese king, limited his authority, and forced him to permit private merchants to profit from trade. The Portuguese monarchy eventually realized the value of private enterprise to its economy and sought to encourage private investment, going so far as to limit executive authority. These findings rebut the view that "initial institutions" account for the divergence between England and Portugal because they suggest that the Portuguese government did not prevent private enterprise.

Complicating the Story

In this section, I present examples from Portuguese history that complicate prior assertions that the Portuguese monarchy exercised unlimited control on overseas trade and prevented all private enterprise. In separate instances between the 15th and 17th Centuries, the Portuguese monarchy (1) capitulated to powerful merchants who sought to conduct business in Portugal and (2) supported the growth of private enterprise through the establishment of joint-stock companies and other pro-market reforms.

For the first of the two subsections that follow I draw substantially from Donald Lach's Asia in the Making of Europe, Volume I: The Century of Discovery, published by the University of Chicago Press in 1994. Though a secondary source, Lach's work described events that were ignored in AJR's argument, and which complicated the prevailing narrative about Portugal's monarch as an all-powerful ruler. For that reason, I describe Lach's findings in the following subsection. In the latter of the two subsections I rely instead on primary sources from the period found either in digital archives or directly quoted in secondary sources.

When Merchants Pushed Back

Following the discovery of the trans-Atlantic route to India, the King of Portugal, Dom Manuel I, sought to profit from direct trade with Asia. In order to do so, he had to supply a fleet of ships to make the long journey. The first post-da Gama voyage to India was that of Pedro Alvarez Cabral, who in 1500 sailed to Calicut to trade with the Indians for goods to resell in Europe. The king financed this voyage, but not alone. Instead he partnered with private investors to fund a fleet of 13 ships, of which private investors paid for 3. The investors included one Genoese merchant, two Florentine merchants, and two Portuguese noblemen. After the voyage was complete, and the spices sold, investors received a profit of more than 100 percent of their investment, net of royal customs.20

After 1500, private involvement in overseas trade increased. A prominent German merchant company, the Fuggers, moved some of their operations from Venice to Lisbon. There, in 1503, they led a coalition of other German merchant businesses in the city to negotiate with King Manuel I to gain permission to establish business in Portugal. Under the agreement, the monarchy permitted them to import central European wares to Portugal for a duty of 10% and to export Asian goods purchased in Lisbon duty free, albeit at regulated prices. The merchants had leverage in negotiations because they controlled a supply of shipbuilding materials harvested in Central Europe that were scarce in Portugal. They also had access to metals such as silver, copper, and lead, which the Portuguese King depended on in order to reach and then trade with the polities in India. The king depended on this merchant support in order to conduct any trade with India.21

As opportunities for investment grew, merchants, including the Fuggers, sought to send ships from Lisbon to trade independently in India and then sell spices in Europe themselves. They wanted to increase their profit by circumventing the government as the middleman. For precisely that reason, the king sought to prevent them from doing business. However, the monarch faced constraints. For one, his own expeditions to India were expensive and he could not finance them alone. Lacking such financing, he chose to permit merchants to send their own ships to India and share in the profits when they sold them in Europe. In 1504, he permitted a German-Italian merchant coalition to send their own ships to trade for spices in India. When they returned in 1506 ready to sell their spices in Europe, Manuel, fearing a drop in the price of spices, attempted to renege on the agreement. He realized that an increase in supply of spices in Europe would decrease the value of his own inventory. The merchants protested against this violation and threatened to cease any future business with the king. The crown at first offered to let the merchants sell their goods only after he had emptied his inventory. Not content with this offer, the merchants held out until 1507, when the king capitulated to permit the merchants to sell their spices in Lisbon. Even with taxes and delays, the merchants netted a profit of between 150 and 175 percent.22

Then in 1517, the king issued a decree that further entrenched his dependence on public-private partnership, "in recognition of his inability to halt" non-crown imports.23 The decree permitted all Portuguese subjects to trade in the products of India so long as duties were paid upon import. It also made the Portuguese port of Malacca open to all traders, so long as tariffs were paid. As competition from merchants increased, the price of spices from India fell and increased pressure on the king by reducing the value of his inventory and thus his potential source of financing. In this tightening market the king faced weakening finances and in 1578 decided to sell contracts for the operation of his own overseas voyages. These contracts granted merchants trading rights in exchange for paying a share of the profits to the government. The Portuguese trade with Asia operated through these contracts from 1578 through at least 1591.24

Thus, while the Portuguese king sought to maximize the crown's revenue following the discovery of new routes to India, he depended on private partnership to operate. Merchants demonstrated their importance when they contributed to the voyage of Cabral in 1500 and reaped a profit. They again exercised their clout over the crown in 1507 when they refused to let the king halt the sale of their inventory. Finally, the king revealed his weakening position as the 16th Century wore on when he issued a decree permitting private commerce in 1517 and ultimately issued contracts for the conduct of trans-Atlantic trade starting in 1578, unable to conduct trans-Atlantic Trade independently. The threat of competition and the need for financing placed de facto limits on the king's power to exercise control.

When the Crown Tried to Help

By the 17th Century, the nexus of trans-Atlantic Trade activity began to shift away from Portugal. As knowledge of the trans-Atlantic trade routes spread, competition with Portugal grew. England and the Netherlands especially asserted themselves. In 1577, England's Francis Drake circumnavigated the globe. In 1599, the English East India Company formed, followed in 1602 by the Dutch East India Company. By 1600, the Fuggers had left Lisbon for Antwerp.25

Against these challenges, a Portuguese merchant proposed a pro-market initiative. In the early 16th Century, a Portuguese merchant, Duarte Gomes Solis began to advocate in favor of a privately-funded joint stock company in Portugal. In 1628, he published the "Argument in favor of the East India Company." The treatise argued that the establishment of a privately-funded joint stock company would be a "total and complete remedy" to Portugal's declining position in the trans-Atlantic economy.26 Rather than punish Solis for proposing an enterprise that might compete with the monarchy—as the AJR narrative might lead us to suppose—the king supported the idea of a joint-stock company and encouraged municipalities and private merchants to invest.27 In 1624 Dom Jorge Mascarenhas, a Portuguese nobleman and the president of Lisbon's municipal council, established a committee to help organize the establishment of a joint-stock India Company in Portugal.

In 1626 King of Portugal Philip III sent a letter to that commission expressing support for the company.28 The king wanted to invest in the company and he took notable measures to limit fears of his influence. He issued a decree that stated that the rights inherent in his stock would be equivalent to that of any other private investor:

I shall have only the rights of a private person ... and not the privileges of the king, fiscal or royal treasury, and will be subject to all its laws and agreements without the power to do anything against it.29

In this decree, the king placed limits on his authority, and presented himself as a subject of laws. However despite these limits on executive authority, and the effort from private individuals (like Solis) and the monarchy alike, trans-Atlantic ventures from Portuguese ports failed to stimulate private investment.30

In the 18th Century, the Portuguese government tried again to stimulate private investment into the country. In 1755, King Jose I's chief deputy, the Marquis of Pombal, led a market liberalization campaign at the helm of the Portuguese government. Before taking office, Pombal had lived in London for five years as a diplomat to England. During that time he studied the sources of Britain's success. His observations there convinced him that England depended on private enterprise for its success, and that a European economy needed private investment to succeed.31 Pombal shared these views with a circle of like-minded thinkers in Portuguese society.32 In a letter he expounded the virtues of commercial companies for their enrichment of private merchants:

One of the great public utilities that the commercial company will bring, ... [is the] restor[ation] to the marketplaces of Portugal and Brazil the commissions of which they were deprived, and which are the principal substance of commerce, and the means by which there could be established the great merchant houses which had been lacking in Portugal.33

Pombal desired large private businesses for Portugal. Like Solis, he viewed England and the Netherlands with envy, and sought to replicate their model to create growth in Portugal.

In 1755, the king placed Pombal in charge of the government. That year, Pombal granted exclusive rights of commerce and navigation in parts of Brazil to a private company, the Company of Para and Maranhao, to trade in Brazil, following the model of successful monopoly companies in England. Later, he went on to establish at least four private monopoly companies for trade domestically within Portugal and in Brazil, Africa, and India, including another attempt at a Portuguese India Company. He intended for these private companies to serve as opportunities for private investors to profit from trade in Portugal.

However, as with Solis's India company, Pombal's initiatives largely floundered. The companies that Pombal supported failed to attract much investment or perform profitably. Many of them lost money and dissolved even before the end of his tenure.34

Under Pombal, Portugal's government did the opposite of what the analysis of AJR would suggest. Far from preventing private enterprise, the Portuguese government invested in it. But private investment still failed to materialize in Portugal, even while it boomed elsewhere in Europe.

A Geographic Explanation

While the narrative of Portuguese history advanced by AJR says that the Portuguese monarchy quashed private investment, Dom Manuel's partnerships with private investors, Solis's initiative for an East India company, and the companies launched during the Pombaline era suggest that private investment in trans-Atlantic trade was weak in Portugal despite government support, not because of monarchical opposition.

If absolutism does not fully explain the decline of Portugal's disappointing economic performance among Atlantic Europe, what does? What else caused Portugal to fall so far behind, especially given its initial advantage?

The answer lies in Portugal's location. In this section I show that Portugal's geography separated it from the largest and the most profitable markets of Europe.

A Framework for Thinking About Growth

Below, I provide a conceptual framework from which to interpret the role of geography in a country's economic output. I illustrate a standard model of economic growth in which output is a function of technology and capital. I then illustrate how in this model, geography, in addition to technology and institutions, can affect output.

In order to evaluate the level of economic growth between countries consider the following standard model of country-level production.

Yi = Ai(Ki)     (1)

Y represents output, A represents a productivity function for the inputs of capital, K, all for country i. Capital is broadly defined to represent all forms—physical and human capital, natural resources, labor, and so forth. In the context of Early Modern Europe, a country may increase its capital by acquiring more land and increasing its agricultural capacity. Alternatively, the population may grow and supply more laborers, or the existing population may work more hours.

Equation (1) also shows that, holding capital constant, a country may alter its output by increasing its productivity (represented by the function A in Equation 1). Therefore, consider the following function of productivity:

Ak,i = f(geography, technology, institutions)     (2)

Which shows that Ak,i, or the productivity function of capital, k, in country i, is determined by some function, f, of geography, technology, and institutions. Geographical factors across countries may affect the productivity of industry through, for example, abundant rainfall that provides a positive effect on the productivity of a certain crop. Geography may alternatively increase productivity if it provides navigable waterways that reduce transportation costs. Furthermore, technology, such as the innovation of a new shipping route, advancement in crop rotation, or the invention of a new machine for a factory could all increase productivity. Finally, institutions can also affect productivity through the determination of taxes, or the security of property rights. For example, a government reform that establishes greater security of property rights may induce individuals to work harder towards profits that they know they can keep.

In addition to cross-sectional variation, productivity differences across countries at a given point in time may lead to divergence over time. This may occur for two reasons. First, because businesses have an incentive to operate where productivity is the highest, they may move operations from one region to another if they can obtain greater productivity by doing so. In other words, an increase in productivity in period t incentivizes businesses to immigrate for period t + 1. Second, holding capital and capital per capita constant across countries, investors in places with higher productivities will have a greater incentive to innovate because the size of the market in the country with higher productivity will have more wealth, all things equal. Schmookler (1966) writes "the amount of invention is governed by the extent of the market."35 Therefore because of (1) the incentives to immigrate to high-productivity countries and (2) the greater incentives to innovate in such countries, variations in productivity across countries at a given point in time may lead to divergence in the growth rate of productivity over time.

Equations (1) and (2), along with the potential effect of productivity on cross-country divergence, demonstrates how geography, in addition to institutions and technology, can alter the level of output and the rates of growth in output across countries. In the next subsection, I present data that shows how geography varied across Atlantic European ports in ways that disadvantaged Portugal.

Portugal's Geographic Disadvantages

Geographic characteristics affect agricultural output, the costs of trade, and population density. Henderson et al. (2017) found a strong relationship between the location of population density and the distribution of geographic characteristics that affect agriculture and trade. The authors found that even in today's developed countries (where transportation technology like cars, trains, or planes reduce the costs of transportation and modern construction machines can alter the geography itself) there exists a correlation between the location of geographic amenities that improve the process of transportation and the location of population density. Furthermore, they find that this relationship is especially strong in regions that developed before the advent of modern transportation technology, such as those of Western Europe.36

Geographic characteristics imposed two related disadvantages in the regions around Portugal's main Atlantic Ports, Lisbon and Porto. First, the regions around Portugal's ports provided less crop-suitable land and imposed higher costs on transportation. This created a disincentive for investment in Portugal, by imposing higher costs on the conduct of business. Secondly, Portugal's ports were further from the consumers than ports in other Atlantic Trader countries.

Below, I present data on geographic characteristics that affect a place's amenability to commerce: ruggedness, access to rivers and harbors, land suitability to crop cultivation, and access to consumers. These geographic characteristics inhibited Portugal's economic development.

Rugged Terrain

The ruggedness of land imposes costs on economic development by making it harder to traverse and cultivate.37 Nunn and Puga (2012) evaluated these impacts and summarized them as follows:

Ruggedness has a number of effects on income that all regions of the world experience. The best-established of these are the contemporary negative effects of ruggedness. Irregular terrain makes cultivation difficult. On steep slopes, erosion becomes a potential hazard, and the control of water, such as irrigation, becomes much more difficult. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (1993), when slopes are greater than 2 degrees, the benefits of cultivation often do not cover the necessary costs, and when slopes are greater than 6 degrees, cultivation becomes impossible. In addition, because of the very high costs involved in earthwork, building costs are much greater when terrain is irregular (Rapaport & Snickars, 1999; Nogales, Archondo-Callao, & Bhandari, 2002). As well, transportation over irregular terrain is slower and more costly.38

The authors define ruggedness as the topographical variation in land within a given area and create an index that identifies this characteristic around the world.

Nearby Customers

If all geographic terrain were the same, the most lucrative ports to which to deliver commodities would be those with the greatest number of consumers ready to buy the goods. Geographic characteristics could also affect population sizes, by reducing the amount of people that a given area can sustain.40

In addition to the effect of geography through physical features, geography affected development of Atlantic Traders through proximity to customers for goods traded across the Atlantic. To evaluate the effect of access to markets I evaluate the variation in population size in the regions around the Atlantic ports. I use recently compiled data from Ruba et al. (2016), who digitized estimates of urban populations from Chandler (1987) and geocoded these records. I consider their "initial" population at the outset of the period of Atlantic trade, defined as the average of population records from Ruba et al. (2016) for each city between 1400 and 1500.

These estimates include only urban settlements. They do not count, for example, households of agricultural workers in unincorporated zones. However, assuming that the relative distribution of populations outside of counted cities is not significantly correlated with the number of cities or size of cities, the absence of these records would not significantly affect the comparison of population from region to region. Furthermore, the possibility of measurement error arising from the exclusion of unincorporated zones would bias against my conclusion (that Portugal had poor access to consumers) under the additional assumption that the size of the population in unincorporated areas is positively correlated with size of the incorporated population across space.

Second, that the initial population around these ports was smaller than those of many other ports in Atlantic Europe. Portugal's port cities tended to have inferior geographies, both for producing crops and facilitating trade. Additionally, the population estimates showed that Lisbon and Porto had fewer initial customers in their surrounding regions.

The initial population differences are especially noteworthy because they suggest that even before the advent of trans-Atlantic trade, the ports in Portugal were especially remote. Viewing the population size as the number of potential customers for Asian imports, these initial populations suggest that in the 15th Century, ports outside of Portugal provided many multiples of the number of customers that Lisbon or Porto could offer.

These findings suggest that Portugal had a smaller market to provide to merchants, and that even if it could have provided as many customers, it would have imposed higher transportation costs on the delivery of goods to those consumers. These two characteristics placed Portugal at a disadvantage relative to all other ports in Atlantic Europe, except Spain. Ports outside of Portugal provided more consumers and easier access to them. For these two reasons, the evidence suggests that shipping goods directly to English or the Low Countries reduced the costs associated with supplying goods, increased the potential profits, and stimulated private investment.

Regression Results

To evaluate how these findings relate to AJR's conclusions about the role of institutions, I replicate their model and incorporate my new data on geographic characteristics of Atlantic Traders. This section presents the results of that analysis.

AJR evaluate the role of institutions on economic growth in the following model, shown in Equation (3) below, in which u represents the urbanization rate (a proxy for economic growth) in country j at time t. Then d and δ represent country and year fixed effects respectively, WE represents a dummy for whether or not the country is in Western Europe, while lnAT represents the volume of Atlantic trade. PAT is a dummy representing the existence of an Atlantic Port. I represents the institutions of each country, taken either as the countries' initial institutions (i.e. those in effect at the dawn of Atlantic trade) or times taken as the institutions in each year times a dummy γ denoting each year.

ujt = dt + δt
+ Σt≤1500 αt · WEj · dt + β · lnATt · PATj
+ Σt≤1150 γt · Ij,1415 · dt
+ η · lnATt · PATj · Ij,1415 + εjt     (3)

The fifth term is the variable of interest. The estimated coefficient on this variable represents the degree to which the quality of institutions at the outset of Atlantic Trade determined a country's ability to seize the opportunity to grow from such trade. AJR summarize as follows: "The variable lnATt × PATj × Ij,1415 tests the hypothesis of interest. A significant coefficient implies that there were divergent paths taken by countries with different initial institutions, but this divergence relates significantly to whether they took advantage of the opportunities presented by Atlantic trade."41

AJR measure the quality of initial institutions using an index produced by Polity IV, which grades each government on a 1 to 7 scale, in which 1 represents a government with no constraints on its executive while 7 represents a government where the executive is completely subordinate to a representative body and the rule of law.

My argument suggests that institutions do not account for the divergence between England and Holland on the one hand and France, Portugal, and Spain on the other. Geography instead accounts for this divergence. In the preceding section I have shown how geographic differences stacked up in such a way as to inhibit investment in the Iberian Peninsula, and benefit it in the Low Countries and England.

To test this theory—that geography matters instead of institutions—I include geographic characteristics in the econometric analysis. I repeat AJR's model estimation shown in Equation (3) above. I then include my geography index alongside AJR's variables in the following equation:

ujt = dt + δt
+ Σt≤1500 αt · WEj · dt + β · lnATt · PATj
+ Σt≤1150 γt · Ij,1415 · dt
+ η · lnATt · PATj · Ij,1415
+ η · lnATt · PATj · Gj + εjt     (4)

where all of the variables are the same as in AJR's model except for the inclusion of an index of geographic characteristics, G, in the same format and in addition to the index of initial institutions. The index is taken as the geographic index from Exhibit 7, which represents the mean of the percentile of each variable used to estimate geographic amenity towards development.

The coefficients on the variables of η · lnATt · PATj · Gj and η · lnATt · PATj · Ij,1415 test my hypothesis. I hypothesize (1) that controlling for geographic characteristics reduces or eliminates the positive effect of institutions on development and (2) that geographic characteristics themselves have a positive effect.

Exhibit 8 shows these results. Columns (1) and (2) show a replication of the model from AJR (2005), which matches their results. Columns (3) and (4) show the results when I include my measure of geographic characteristics: the coefficient on the variable representing the interaction of institutions and the volume of Atlantic trade becomes negative in the case when regressed on the urbanization rate, positive but not statistically significant when regressed on GDP, and negative but not statistically significant when regressed on the constraint on the executive. In contrast, the coefficient on the variable that measures geographic characteristics is positive and statistically significant at the 5 to 1 percent level. The results suggest that an increase in the percentile of any of the geographic characteristics would increase the urbanization rate by 1 percent and the log GDP by 2.5 percent.

Notably, their inclusion alters the estimated effect of institutions that forms a central pillar of AJR (2005)'s findings. Geography in Practice

The preceding econometric analysis suggests a causal relationship between Portugal's geography and its economic performance. In this section, I evaluate historical evidence to support the existence of such a causal relationship. I point to three specific examples to make this case. First, existing research on Portuguese agriculture shows that the country endured weak agricultural output in the period of concern. Second, the cost of transportation in Early Modern Europe reflected the advantages of water-borne navigation. Third, investments in office and factory sitings made by both merchants and the Portuguese government recognized the geographic differences between Portugal and the Low Countries.

Recent scholarly work on Portuguese economic history has revealed that low agricultural productivity limited output in Early Modern Portugal and in turn the size of the population. Santos (2005) evaluated the relationship between wheat harvests and population growth in the Evora region of Portugal. He found a positive relationship between the price of staple foods and mortality rates. Santos argued that the observed "price increases were supply-driven, rather than demand-driven," and that the productivity of land constrained growth rather than the other way around.42 Reis (2016) compiled a data series that measured gross agricultural output in Portugal between the 16th and 18th Centuries, which revealed that Portugal's agricultural output declined between 1500 and 1700. Despite an upward trend beginning in the 18th Century, the country's agriculture "was unable, [to] keep up with the even stronger concomitant growth of population."43 Subsequent research by Reis (2017) found that Portugal's agricultural structure played a central role in shaping the distribution of income in the country. "The highly labour-intensive type of cultivation that spread after 1600," Reis writes, "helps explain this continuous absorption of labour."44 Together, these studies present a world in Early Modern Portugal in which agricultural production constrained economic growth.

Geography also constrained economic development through impediments to transportation. The difficulty of transporting goods led to corresponding increases in commodity prices. Dittmar (2011) noted that "Transportation costs—especially for heavier products and overland transport—were exceedingly high. Grain transported 200 kilometers overland could see its price rise by nearly 100 percent. While the early modern period saw major developments in the international trade in grain, most cities remained heavily reliant on the provision of foodstuffs from within a circle of 20 to 30 kilometers which avoided heavy transport costs and the risks of reliance on foreign supplies."45 The significance of rugged terrain to economic development was also highlighted by Nunn and Puga (2012). Their work showed that rugged terrain hampered economic development by increasing the costliness of transportation.46 Furthermore, Adam Smith commented on the significance of commodity transportation in 1776, writing in Wealth of Nations:

[I]t is upon the sea-coast, and along the banks of navigable rivers, that industry of every kind naturally begins to subdivide and improve itself, and it is frequently not till a long time after that those improvements extend themselves to the inland parts of the country. ... Six or eight men, [...] by the help of water-carriage, can carry and bring back in the same time the same quantity of goods between London and Edinburgh, as fifty broad-wheeled wagons, attended by a hundred men, and drawn by four hundred horses. [...] Were there no communication between [two] places, therefore, but by land-carriage as no goods could be transported from the one to the other, except such whose price was very considerable in proportion to their weight, [...] there could be little or no commerce of any kind between the distant parts of the world. [...]

Since such, therefore, are the advantages of water-carriage, it is natural that the first improvements of art and industry should be made where this conveniency opens the whole world for a market to the produce of every sort of labor [...] [T]he extent of their market, therefore, must for a long time be in proportion to the riches and populousness of that country.47

Smith drew evidence from Britain for his example, but his argument concerned an economic reality that applied universally. He identified that the costliness of transporting commodities overland gave a natural advantage to locations near navigable water. Furthermore, he believed that this reality helped to explain the geographic distribution of economic activity.

Finally, the location of investment by both merchants and the Portuguese government indicated that geographic factors influenced the siting of economic activity. When Lisbon emerged as a launching point for trans-Atlantic trade, the Fuggers, a German merchant house, established an office in Lisbon by 1504 to serve as a base to negotiate with the Portuguese crown. Because water-borne access of an Atlantic port connected Europe to India, Lisbon itself offered an advantage. However, Antwerp offered access to both the Atlantic and superior water-borne transportation within Europe. Accordingly, the Fuggers eventually moved operations from Lisbon to Antwerp, where a larger population, less rugged terrain, and more navigable waterways offered advantages for economic activity.48 Furthermore, the Portuguese government itself invested in Antwerp early on in the period of trans-Atlantic trade. By 1508 the Portuguese government operated a distribution center in Antwerp, called the Feitoria de Flandres.49 This outpost of the Portuguese crown revealed a recognition at the beginning of the spice trade that a supply depot in the Low Countries offered advantageous proximity to markets.50 Both the government and private enterprises sought the most profitable market to conduct trade. As they did so, geographic reality, not institutions, determined where that activity would locate.

So What Does This Mean?

In this paper, I have argued in favor of a new interpretation of Portugal's economic history between 1500 and 1800. My argument relies on two main sets of findings. First, I present examples from Portugal's history in which, contrary to prior claims in the literature, private enterprise in Portugal did participate in Atlantic Trade. The government did not permanently exercise absolute control nor deliberately extinguish all business activity. On multiple occasions the crown capitulated to private investors, and later sought to encourage private investment not prevent it. Despite this, I confirm the suggestion that private enterprise in Portugal was weak. Therefore, I point to a second set of findings that suggest that geographic characteristics placed Portugal at a disadvantage. The geography of the regions surrounding Portugal's ports imposed higher costs on commercial activity and inferior access to consumers as compared to other ports, notably London and those of the Low Countries. Taken together, these findings suggest that geography provides a stronger explanation for variation among Atlantic traders than institutions. Geography, rather than institutions, accounts for the economic decline of Portugal relative to other Atlantic Traders. In the next two subsections, I discuss the strengths and limitations of my findings.

The King Wasn't the Problem

The first part of my article seeks to challenge a prior argument that holds institutions as the central determinant of Portugal's economic fate. In presenting this challenge, this study adds to other current research, especially that of Henriques and Palma (2019), which revises our assessment of early modern institutions. Below, I revisit the AJR argument and then demonstrate how my findings challenge it.

AJR utilized a narrative created by prior historians of Portugal, that the Portuguese monarchy exercised absolute control of trans-Atlantic commerce, and as a result stifled all economic development, putting it at a disadvantage relative to the rest of Atlantic Europe and especially England and the Netherlands. This element of Portuguese historiography suggests that the absence of constraints on executive authority allowed the Portuguese monarch to control all trade and capture all the associated profits, and thus ensure that no private entrepreneurs could enrich themselves, nor deliver political reforms.

The examples brought forth in this article do not support this view. Instead, my findings suggest that when the king sought to monopolize trade, merchants in Lisbon successfully constrained him, and when he sought to stimulate private industry, his attempts failed in spite of his support.

In the case of the merchants that migrated to Lisbon in the early 16th Century, like the Fuggers, private entrepreneurs utilized their bargaining power—access to capital and to particularly important metals from central Europe—to force the king to make concessions. In one notable instance, the monarchy attempted to rebuff the merchants' request to send their own ships to India, but, constrained by merchant resistance, later capitulated. Then, following another attempt by the king to renege on this agreement, merchants threatened to hold out on future business. In so doing, they gained further rights, and demonstrated the dependence of the king on the private sector. They showed that the king's power was limited. As the king continued to lose control, he made additional concessions to allow private traders to conduct business in India, and in Portugal.

Furthermore, after the 16th Century, Portuguese monarchs recognized that they needed more private investment. Rather than view countries like England or the Netherlands, where private investment was strong, as cautionary tales, the Portuguese court recognized private industry as a fundamental component in success. Consequently, the monarchy sought on two occasions to generate a Portuguese version of the Dutch and English East India Companies. However in both attempts to stimulate such investment, the companies failed.

A critic of my argument might challenge my interpretation of Portugal's attempts to spur India companies. After all, both initiatives were driven by the government to some degree. In fact, the primary investor in the 17th Century East India Company was the king himself, and the 18th Century attempt was the result of initiative by the king's deputy. In this light, one might contend that government intervention in these companies may have doomed them, and they thus seem more like exceptions that prove the rule of government overreach in Portugal.

While I cannot rule out the possibility that the mere involvement of the crown in the initiatives may have reduced enthusiasm for investment, the contemporary sources I cite, including quotes from the king and Pombal, both suggest that their intention was to stimulate rather than stifle private investment by doing everything in their power to create profits for investors. In both the case of the 1628 Portuguese India Company, and the Pombaline-era version, supporters of the companies identified the generation of private profit as the central goal of the companies' formation. Recognizing the importance of this private-sector profit motive in the 1628 India Company, the king went so far as to commit to possess "only the rights of a private person" as a stock-holder. Furthermore, in the second attempt in the Pombaline era, Pombal specifically identified the generation of profits for "the marketplaces of Portugal" and the emergence of "great merchant houses" as the central goal of the initiative. Neither of these motivations suggest a government that wielded unlimited authority to stifle the private sector. They suggest the opposite: a weak government attempting, albeit unsuccessfully, to stimulate private-sector activity.

Furthermore, this study's conclusion that institutions do not fully account for Portugal's economic performance during the period of Atlantic Trade aligns closely with other recent scholarly work that challenges the role of institutions and especially the findings of Henriques and Palma (2019), which, considering quantitative measurements of institutional quality, finds no clear inhibition imposed on Portugal by institutions. Contributing to such literature, the present study offers a strong challenge to arguments that place institutions at the center of Portugal's laggard economic performance during the period of Atlantic Trade.

The events that I point to in Portuguese history—government concessions to merchants in the 16th Century, and failed attempts to stimulate private investment in the 17th and 18th Centuries—challenge the view that the Portuguese crown wielded free reign over the economy in order to stifle private industry. Furthermore, they show that private investment and private profit did exist in Portugal's overseas trade. The power of merchants in Portugal constrained the power of the king, and the crown envied rather than detested the success of merchants in other countries.

Wrong Place, Wrong Time

If not institutions, then what else could have accounted for Portugal's decline? In Section 4 of this article, I argued that what distinguished Portugal was not the inferiority of its institutions, but that of its geography. It lacked geographic assets that were available to other port cities in Europe and this made it an inferior place to do business, with higher costs of production and fewer opportunities for revenue. I argue in favor of this view by pointing to (1) the costs that geography imposes, (2) the variation in market size, (3) the results when including the geography index in AJR's model.

Geography in Portugal imposed costs on industry. If traders chose to unload their spices in Lisbon or Porto, they would have faced terrain that was more rugged and provided less access to water-based transport than in Antwerp, London, or Amsterdam. These characteristics would have increased the costs involved in their activity, through increases to labor or capital costs or lost time, and decreased their profits.51

Additionally, the region around Lisbon had a population much smaller than that around the cities of the Low Countries and England. This meant that even if Portugal's geography did provide transportation advantages, there would have been fewer customers to buy goods in comparison to prominent ports in the Low Countries or London.

While AJR account for latitude and several country-level fixed effects, they do not include land suitability, availability of navigable waterways, ruggedness, or regional population size as variables in their econometric analysis, all of which my evidence suggests would be important determinants in the location of economic activity.

After Portugal pioneered new routes to Asia, the country enjoyed a temporary technological advantage over other European countries via innovative shipping technology and sea routes, but it was always hindered by its geography. Overseas traders sought to make profits by delivering foreign goods like spices to customers in Europe. The data presented in this article showed that when merchants could choose to conduct trade in Amsterdam, Antwerp, or London on the one hand, and Lisbon or Porto on the other hand, they faced a choice between lower transportation costs and more customers in the former, and higher transportation costs and fewer customers in the latter. Therefore, even if institutions were equivalent across all of the Atlantic Trader countries, Portugal would still have had a disadvantage. Once countries such as England and the Netherlands could copy Portugal's shipping technology and routes, Portugal's inherent geographic disadvantages (high transportation costs and a remote location) meant that trans-Atlantic shipping was more profitable when it docked at other ports. As a result, economic activity moved to England and the Low Countries, where merchants could put Portugal's innovation to more profitable use.

Conclusion

In this essay, I sought to evaluate why Portugal's economy failed to keep up with that of other Atlantic Traders despite the fact that Portuguese mariners made the initial innovations that enriched much of Atlantic Europe.

To understand why this decline occurred, I collected evidence on the behavior of the Portuguese monarchy in the period of Atlantic trade and on the variation in the distribution of geographic characteristics around Atlantic ports in Western Europe.

In light of my findings, I argue that geography, rather than institutions, accounted for the country's relative decline. To do so, I first showed that the Portuguese monarchy frequently made concessions to private individuals and sought to stimulate rather than stifle private-sector investment. I then showed how geographic characteristics that affect transportation costs and the distribution of populations in Europe disadvantaged Portugal.

The evidence presented in this article challenges prior claims that government institutions account for Portugal's decline and shows that geographic characteristics played an important role in Portugal's economic performance. In doing so, it joins other current research contributions, such as that of Henriques and Palma (2019), in revising our understanding of the role of institutions in early modern economic growth in Europe. Geographic characteristics warrant greater research in order to achieve a full explanation of the divergence among Atlantic Traders after 1500.
